Canada’s $6.6 Trillion Deposit Market Is Fragmenting Into Three Tiers — and OSFI M4 Shows Exactly Where You Stand

Non-retail deposits surged 9% while retail barely moved at 0.9%. The 2022–23 GIC vintage is still rolling off. The institutions that captured the most growth in this cycle are the most exposed in the next one. Here is what the data says.

By Deep Bando · August 2026 · 14 min read · Source: OSFI M4 Monthly Banking Statistics
Why

The headline number looks reassuring. Canada’s deposit liabilities reached $6.6 trillion in 2025, up 6.0% year-over-year, against a total balance sheet of $10 trillion growing at 7.6%. If you are managing a deposit book inside a Canadian financial institution, you are probably reading that as stability.

You should be reading it as a warning.

The aggregate growth rate conceals a structural divergence that changes the competitive picture entirely. Retail unregistered deposits grew 0.9%. Non-retail deposits grew 9.0%, adding $308 billion — and 97% of that growth went to five institutions. The deposit market is not growing evenly. It is concentrating, and the institutions holding most of the new money are holding a book that is structurally more volatile than it appears.

“The institutions that captured 97% of the deposit growth in this cycle face a specific risk: the same rate dynamics that drove institutional money in will drive it out. The question is whether their funding strategy is built around relationship stickiness or rate competitiveness — because only one of those survives a rate reversal.”

I spent twelve years inside the pricing and analytics functions of major Canadian financial institutions. I built the models that set deposit rates, ran the segmentation that targeted deposit campaigns, and analyzed the run-off risk on books that looked healthy until they did not. The OSFI M4 data for 2025 tells a familiar story, and the banks that understand the subtext have a significant strategic advantage heading into 2026 and beyond.

This essay breaks down what that data actually shows: the structural divergence between retail and non-retail growth, the three competitive tiers that are emerging, the mechanics of the GIC vintage maturity problem, and what institutions in each tier should be doing right now.

What

Three structural tiers have emerged — and they have fundamentally different exposure profiles

The most important finding from OSFI M4 is not the aggregate growth rate. It is the competitive fragmentation. The Canadian deposit market is no longer a single competitive arena. It has separated into three distinct strategic tiers, each with a different growth model, a different source of competitive advantage, and a different vulnerability as the rate cycle turns.

Understanding which tier you are in is the starting point for any meaningful deposit strategy conversation. The action implications are entirely different depending on where you sit.

Tier 1: Relationship Banks

RBC, CIBC, and National Bank are the only D-SIBs that grew across nearly every deposit segment simultaneously in 2025. This is not a coincidence and it is not primarily about rate competitiveness. It reflects a structural advantage: their customers hold their primary banking relationship with them. Deposits follow the primary relationship, not the rate. This is the most durable competitive moat in retail financial services, and it is very hard to replicate quickly.

RBC is additionally benefiting from the HSBC Canada acquisition, which brought a substantial base of high-net-worth and internationally mobile clients — a customer profile that generates disproportionate deposit balances and cross-sell value.

Tier 2: Rate-Cycle Banks

TD, BMO, and Scotia grew overall but experienced structural losses in specific segments. Their deposit growth is more contingent on rate competitiveness and cycle timing. When rates were elevated, they captured volume. As the cycle turns, that volume is exposed.

BMO made the most explicit strategic pivot: it shed $27 billion in high-cost term deposits to defend net interest margin, recapturing $7 billion in lower-cost demand deposits. This is a rational NIM-first decision, but it signals that BMO is managing its balance sheet defensively rather than competitively. Scotia is in a structurally similar position, losing registered book to run-off as the 2022–23 GIC vintage matures.

Tier 3: Concentration Challengers

Questbank, Tru Cooperative, Coast Capital, and EQ Bank are not trying to compete with the Big 5 across all segments. They are winning by dominating specific niches. Questbank grew $2.41 billion in non-registered retail deposits with 100% concentration in that segment. Tru Cooperative and Coast Capital grew through merger consolidation. The challenger model succeeds through precision, not breadth.

How

The mechanics: GIC vintage maturity, the demand shift, and institutional seasonality

Understanding the tier structure is the strategic lens. Understanding the mechanics is what makes deposit strategy actionable. Three dynamics are driving the 2025–2026 deposit landscape, and they interact in ways that are not always obvious from the headline numbers.

The GIC vintage problem

In 2022–23, Canadian banks aggressively competed for retail term deposits as rates rose. GICs were booked at 4.5–5.0% — attractive rates that brought in significant volume. That vintage is now maturing. The retail deposit growth that looks like 0.9% year-over-year is largely a recycling problem: the maturities generate gross deposit flows, but the net new money is minimal. Worse, the rate environment into which those deposits are rolling is 150–200 basis points lower than when they were booked.

Banks that did not manage the maturity profile of their GIC book are facing redemption cliffs — concentrated maturity clusters that create funding volatility and NIM pressure simultaneously. BMO’s decision to shed $27 billion in high-cost term deposits is the most visible example of an institution choosing NIM protection over volume retention.

The demand/notice shift

As rates decline, retail investors are moving from fixed-term to demand and notice products. In 2025, non-registered demand deposits grew $44 billion and registered demand deposits grew $13.97 billion. Fixed-term products declined: non-registered GICs fell $24 billion, registered fixed-term (RRSP/TFSA GICs) fell $1.3 billion.

The registered fixed-term stickiness — a decline of only $1.3 billion versus $24 billion in non-registered — is a meaningful finding. RRSP and TFSA holders are less price-sensitive on the downside than non-registered investors. This has implications for how registered products should be priced and positioned as rates continue to fall.

How non-retail strategy differs across the Big 5

The non-retail deposit picture is more nuanced than the aggregate suggests. While 97% of non-retail growth went to the Big 5, the growth strategies within that group are fundamentally different — and those differences will determine NIM performance as the rate cycle continues to turn.

Key Insight

RBC, TD, and Scotia are running a term-heavy institutional strategy (75% fixed-term, 25% demand), which looks strong in a rising-rate environment and becomes a NIM risk as rates fall. BMO’s 50/50 split and CIBC’s demand-led approach (75% demand, 25% fixed-term) position them better for the current environment — but represent deliberately lower volume targets.

The institutional seasonality risk that most deposit strategies ignore

Beyond the structural tier dynamics, OSFI M4 reveals a temporal pattern that creates predictable risk — and predictable opportunity — for institutions that are paying attention.

Institutional deposit maturities cluster at quarter-end. January and March/June/September/December are the periods when the largest volumes of institutional GICs mature and renewal decisions are made. For institutions with concentrated maturity schedules, these are liquidity events that create funding volatility, rate pressure, and management distraction simultaneously.

For competitors, they are acquisition windows. A bank that proactively reaches institutional depositors 30–60 days before their maturity date — with a compelling offer and a streamlined renewal process — can intercept meaningful balances that would otherwise auto-renew with the incumbent. The data shows this is exactly what Questbank and Tru Cooperative have been doing in the retail segment.

What Now

Strategic implications by tier: what the data is telling you to do

The OSFI M4 data does not just describe a market — it prescribes a response. The actions that make sense for a Tier 1 relationship bank are fundamentally different from those facing a Tier 2 rate-cycle institution or a Tier 3 challenger. What follows is not a generic best-practices list. It is what the data specifically suggests for each competitive position.

If you are Tier 1 (Relationship advantage)

Your structural position is strong but not guaranteed. The primary risk is complacency: assuming that relationship stickiness protects you from rate competition indefinitely. It does not. The window during which deposit relationships are most vulnerable is around product maturity events — GIC renewals, anniversary dates, rate review conversations. Tier 3 challengers have identified these windows precisely and are building maturity-driven outreach systems to intercept your customers before you re-engage them.

The Tier 1 response is to weaponize your data advantage. You know more about your customers than any challenger can learn from a single product relationship. Use that knowledge to predict which customers are most likely to shop rates at maturity, and intervene first with a value offer — not just a rate, but the full relationship value proposition.

If you are Tier 2 (Rate-cycle exposure)

The NIM squeeze from the 2022–23 vintage is real, and it is not over. The institutions that booked the most high-rate term deposits have the most exposure to roll-off margin compression. BMO’s decision to shed volume proactively — rather than wait for the book to run off into lower margins — is worth studying. The question is whether you have the analytical infrastructure to identify which specific segments of your book are most NIM-destructive at current rates, and whether you can execute a selective reduction without triggering broader relationship damage.

The Tier 2 imperative is precision over volume. Stop optimizing for deposit growth as a standalone metric and start optimizing for risk-adjusted NIM contribution by segment.

If you are Tier 3 (Concentration challenger)

Your competitive model is working. The data confirms it. But the Tier 3 risk is equally specific: over-concentration creates vulnerability to a single regulatory change, a rate move in your segment, or a Tier 1 institution deciding to compete seriously in your niche. The Questbank and EQ Bank model requires continuous reinvestment in the data capability that makes targeted acquisition economics sustainable — because the moment you stop optimizing at the segment level, your unit economics converge toward the average.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does OSFI M4 data show about Canadian bank deposit growth in 2025?

OSFI M4 data shows Canadian deposit liabilities reached $6.6 trillion (up 6.0% year-over-year) against a total balance sheet of $10 trillion growing at 7.6%. However, growth is highly asymmetric: retail unregistered deposits grew just 0.9%, while non-retail deposits surged 9.0%, adding $308 billion — 97% of which was captured by the Big 5 banks. This structural divergence is the defining feature of the 2025 Canadian deposit landscape.

Why are Canadian retail deposits growing so slowly compared to non-retail?

Retail deposit growth is slowing because it is not coming from new money entering the system. The 2022–23 GIC vintage — booked at 4.5–5.0% — continues to mature through 2025 and 2026, generating deposit rollovers rather than genuine growth. Meanwhile, retail investors are reallocating from fixed-term products into demand and notice deposits as rates decline. Non-retail growth, by contrast, reflects corporations and governments rebuilding liquidity as capital expenditure slows.

Which Canadian banks are winning the 2025 deposit competition?

According to OSFI M4 data, RBC, CIBC, and National Bank are the only D-SIBs delivering broad-based deposit growth across nearly every segment simultaneously. RBC is further benefiting from the HSBC Canada acquisition, bringing a substantial base of high-net-worth and internationally mobile clients. Among challengers, Questbank (+$2.41B), Tru Cooperative (+$2.40B), and Coast Capital (+$2.03B) are gaining share through concentrated segment dominance.

How is the 2022–23 GIC vintage affecting Canadian bank NIM in 2025?

The 2022–23 GIC vintage — booked at peak rates of 4.5–5.0% — is creating a NIM squeeze as it matures into a lower-rate environment. Banks that aggressively grew their GIC book during this period are now repricing those deposits downward, but the transition is creating redemption cliffs. BMO made the strategic decision to shed $27 billion in high-cost term deposits to defend NIM, recapturing $7 billion in lower-cost demand deposits. Banks that did not actively manage this transition face sustained margin pressure through 2026.

How are challenger banks competing for Canadian deposits against the Big 5?

Challenger institutions are winning by concentrating rather than competing broadly. Questbank grew $2.41 billion in non-registered retail deposits with 100% focus on that segment. EQ Bank grew $3 billion in deposits through data-driven customer acquisition targeting specific value segments — combining competitive rate positioning with precision analytics to identify which customers were most likely to switch and at what rate threshold. The challenger model succeeds through niche dominance, not head-to-head competition with the Big 5 across all segments.

What is the difference between registered and non-registered GIC stickiness in Canada?

OSFI M4 data shows that registered fixed-term deposits (RRSP/TFSA GICs) declined by only $1.3 billion in 2025, while non-registered fixed-term deposits fell $24 billion — making registered GICs approximately 18 times stickier on the downside. This reflects the tax-shelter structure of registered accounts, which creates switching friction: moving a registered GIC requires managing contribution room, potential tax implications, and institutional transfer processes. This stickiness has significant implications for deposit pricing strategy in registered products.
